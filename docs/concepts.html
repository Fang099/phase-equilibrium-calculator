The question

Seal a mixture of two liquids in a container and wait. Some of it evaporates, and the liquid and the vapor settle into a steady state. The two phases do not have the same composition: the vapor is richer in whichever compound escapes more easily. That difference is what distillation uses to separate mixtures.

A phase-equilibrium calculation answers this: given part of the state, what is the rest? For example, given the temperature and the composition of the liquid, what is the pressure, and what is the composition of the vapor above it?

The answer depends on how the molecules of the two compounds get along. The rest of this page builds up the quantities that express this, one at a time.

Chemical potential: what has to balance

Heat flows from high temperature to low temperature until the temperatures are equal. Matter behaves the same way with a different quantity: each compound moves from the phase where its chemical potential \(\mu_i\) is higher to the phase where it is lower, until the two are equal.

This comes straight from the fundamental equation of thermodynamics. In its Helmholtz form, for a system with temperature \(T\), volume \(V\) and \(N_i\) moles of each compound,

\[ dA = -S\,dT - P\,dV + \sum_i \mu_i\,dN_i . \]

The chemical potential is the coefficient of \(dN_i\): the change in energy when a little of compound \(i\) is added. Minimizing the energy of two phases that can exchange heat, volume and molecules gives three conditions:

\[ T^{L} = T^{V}, \qquad P^{L} = P^{V}, \qquad \mu_i^{L} = \mu_i^{V} \ \text{ for every compound } i . \]

Everything the calculator does is a way of working out \(\mu_i\) in each phase and finding the state where they match. The models differ only in how they supply \(\mu_i\).

Fugacity: chemical potential in units of pressure

Chemical potential is awkward to work with. It has no absolute value, only differences, and it falls to minus infinity as a compound becomes very dilute. Fugacity is the same information, rewritten so that it behaves like a pressure.

For an ideal gas, the chemical potential depends on pressure in a simple way:

\[ \mu = \mu^{\circ} + RT \ln\frac{P}{P^{\circ}} . \]

For a real gas, a liquid or a solid, this is no longer true. Fugacity \(f\) is defined as the quantity that makes the same formula exact for any substance:

\[ \mu = \mu^{\circ} + RT \ln\frac{f}{P^{\circ}}, \qquad f \to P \ \text{ as } \ P \to 0 . \]

You can read fugacity as an escaping tendency: how hard the molecules of a compound push to leave the phase they are in, measured in bar. The ratio of fugacity to pressure is the fugacity coefficient,

\[ \varphi = \frac{f}{P}, \]

which is 1 for an ideal gas and moves away from 1 as the molecules interact more.

Because \(\mu\) and \(\ln f\) differ only by a constant at a given temperature, equal chemical potentials mean equal fugacities:

\[ \mu_i^{L} = \mu_i^{V} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \hat f_i^{L} = \hat f_i^{V} . \]

At equilibrium, each compound has the same fugacity in every phase. This is the one equation the solver solves. The hat on \(\hat f_i\) means "compound \(i\) in a mixture".

Fugacity in a vapor mixture

In an ideal-gas mixture the fugacity of a compound is its partial pressure, \(y_i P\), where \(y_i\) is its mole fraction in the vapor. A real vapor needs the fugacity coefficient of that compound in the mixture:

\[ \hat f_i^{V} = y_i\,\hat\varphi_i^{V}\,P . \]

Liquids and activity coefficients

A pure liquid in equilibrium with its own vapor has the same fugacity as that vapor. At low pressure the vapor is close to an ideal gas, so the fugacity of a pure liquid is close to its vapor pressure, \(f_i^{L} \approx P_i^{\mathrm{sat}}(T)\).

In a mixture, a compound makes up only a fraction \(x_i\) of the liquid. If the molecules did not care who their neighbors were, its fugacity would simply scale with that fraction. Real mixtures deviate from this, and the activity coefficient \(\gamma_i\) measures by how much:

\[ \hat f_i^{L} = x_i\,\gamma_i\,f_i^{L} . \]
\(\gamma_i = 1\)
An ideal solution. The compound behaves in the mixture as it does when pure.
\(\gamma_i > 1\)
Positive deviation. The molecules are less comfortable among the other compound than among their own kind, so they escape more readily.
\(\gamma_i < 1\)
Negative deviation. Unlike molecules attract each other, which holds them in the liquid.

From equal fugacities to Raoult's law

Set the liquid fugacity equal to the vapor fugacity, with an ideal-gas vapor:

\[ y_i\,P = x_i\,\gamma_i\,P_i^{\mathrm{sat}} . \]

With \(\gamma_i = 1\) this is Raoult's law. So Raoult's law is not a separate rule. It is the equal-fugacity condition with the two simplest models: an ideal solution and an ideal gas. With real activity coefficients it is called the modified Raoult's law. Adding the equation for both compounds gives the total pressure, \(P = x_1\gamma_1 P_1^{\mathrm{sat}} + x_2\gamma_2 P_2^{\mathrm{sat}}\).

The activity coefficient is largest when a compound is surrounded entirely by the other one. That limit, \(\gamma_i^{\infty}\), is the infinite-dilution activity coefficient. For this mixture at 350 K, \(\gamma^{\infty}\) is for ethanol in water and for water in ethanol.

Negative deviation

Acetone and chloroform form a hydrogen bond with each other that neither forms alone. Both activity coefficients fall below 1, the total pressure dips below Raoult's line, and the mixture has a pressure minimum: a maximum-boiling azeotrope.

The van Laar model

Activity coefficients are not chosen freely. They come from one function, the excess Gibbs energy \(G^{E}\): the Gibbs energy of the real mixture minus that of an ideal solution of the same composition. The two are linked by

\[ \frac{G^{E}}{RT} = \sum_i x_i \ln\gamma_i , \qquad \ln\gamma_i = \left(\frac{\partial\,(nG^{E}/RT)}{\partial n_i}\right)_{T,P,n_{j\neq i}} . \]

An activity model is a formula for \(G^{E}\) as a function of composition, with a few parameters fitted to measurements. Because both \(\gamma_1\) and \(\gamma_2\) come from the same function, they automatically satisfy the Gibbs–Duhem equation: when one rises, the other must fall in a fixed way. The calculator uses the van Laar model; setting both of its parameters to zero gives the ideal solution.

\[ \frac{G^{E}}{RT} = \frac{A_{12}A_{21}\,x_1x_2}{A_{12}x_1 + A_{21}x_2} \] \[ \ln\gamma_1 = A_{12}\left(\frac{A_{21}x_2}{A_{12}x_1 + A_{21}x_2}\right)^{2}, \qquad \ln\gamma_2 = A_{21}\left(\frac{A_{12}x_1}{A_{12}x_1 + A_{21}x_2}\right)^{2} \]

Both parameters must have the same sign.

The parameters have a direct meaning. Setting \(x_1 \to 0\) gives \(\ln\gamma_1^{\infty} = A_{12}\), and \(x_2 \to 0\) gives \(\ln\gamma_2^{\infty} = A_{21}\). Each parameter is the logarithm of an infinite-dilution activity coefficient.

Activity coefficients change with temperature, so the van Laar parameters must too. van Laar derived his model from the van der Waals equation, and in that derivation the excess Gibbs energy comes entirely from the energy of mixing, which makes \(G^{E}/RT\), and with it each \(A_{ij}\), proportional to \(1/T\). The database uses this form with a constant added:

\[ A_{ij}(T) = a_{ij} + \frac{b_{ij}}{T} \qquad (T \text{ in K}). \]

\(b_{ij}\) carries the temperature dependence. For most pairs it is positive: the mixture moves closer to ideal as it gets hotter.

Where the parameters come from

Parameters are fitted to measured vapor–liquid data. The largest collection of such data, the DECHEMA Vapor–Liquid Equilibrium Data Collection, fits each measured data set with several models, but of those fits only the NRTL parameters are openly available (in the ChemSep parameter library). The van Laar parameters in this project's database are therefore regressed from them: for each pair, NRTL gives \(\ln\gamma_1\) and \(\ln\gamma_2\) across all compositions at temperatures spanning the two boiling points, and \(a_{12}, b_{12}, a_{21}, b_{21}\) are fitted to all of those values at once.

For the pairs with van Laar parameters, the root-mean-square error in \(\ln\gamma\) is for the median pair and at most . The fitted values agree with a widely reproduced table of van Laar constants (see Reference). The calculator does not offer van Laar for a pair with no database entry.

The sliders change \(A_{12}\) and \(A_{21}\) at a reference temperature, the mean boiling point of the pair, and keep the database's \(b_{ij}\). Unticking "Vary with temperature" holds them constant instead.

Equations of state

An activity model describes only the liquid. An equation of state describes both phases at once. It gives pressure as a function of temperature, molar volume \(v\) and composition. The cubic equations used here share one form:

\[ P = \frac{RT}{v-b} - \frac{a(T)}{(v+\delta_1 b)(v+\delta_2 b)} \]

The first term is the ideal gas law with the volume reduced by \(b\), the space the molecules themselves take up. The second term lowers the pressure because molecules attract each other, with strength \(a\). The two equations differ in the constants \(\delta_1, \delta_2\) and in whether \(a\) changes with temperature:

Equation\(\delta_1\)\(\delta_2\)\(a\) depends on \(T\)Needs per compound
van der Waals00no\(T_c, P_c\)
Peng–Robinson\(1+\sqrt2\)\(1-\sqrt2\)yes\(T_c, P_c, \omega\)

\(a\) and \(b\) come from the critical temperature and pressure. The acentric factor \(\omega\) tunes \(a(T)\) so that the equation reproduces the compound's vapor pressure. For a mixture, the pure-compound values are combined with mixing rules, and one binary interaction parameter \(k_{12}\) adjusts the attraction between unlike molecules:

\[ a = \sum_i\sum_j x_i x_j \sqrt{a_i a_j}\,(1-k_{ij}), \qquad b = \sum_i x_i b_i . \]

From the equation to fugacity

Pressure as a function of \(T\) and \(V\) is exactly what the Helmholtz form of the fundamental equation needs. Integrating the difference between the real pressure and the ideal-gas pressure over volume gives the residual Helmholtz energy, and one derivative of that gives the fugacity coefficient of each compound:

\[ \frac{A^{\mathrm{res}}}{RT} = -\int_{\infty}^{V}\left(\frac{P}{RT} - \frac{N}{V'}\right)dV', \qquad \ln\hat\varphi_i = \left(\frac{\partial\,(A^{\mathrm{res}}/RT)}{\partial N_i}\right)_{T,V,N_{j\neq i}} - \ln Z . \]

Here \(Z = Pv/RT\) is the compressibility factor. At a given temperature and pressure the cubic can have three roots for the volume. The smallest is the liquid, the largest is the vapor. The same formula then gives the fugacity in both phases, and equilibrium reads

\[ x_i\,\hat\varphi_i^{L}\,P = y_i\,\hat\varphi_i^{V}\,P . \]

An equation of state implies activity coefficients too: \(\gamma_i = \hat\varphi_i^{L}(\text{mixture}) / \varphi_i^{L}(\text{pure } i)\). The calculator reports these, so the two kinds of model can be compared on the same plot.

Reading the diagrams

Pxy: pressure against composition at one temperature

The bubble curve gives the pressure at which a liquid of composition \(x\) starts to boil. The dew curve gives the pressure at which a vapor of composition \(y\) starts to condense. Above the bubble curve everything is liquid, below the dew curve everything is vapor, and between them the two phases coexist. A horizontal line across the two-phase region joins a liquid to the vapor in equilibrium with it.

Txy: temperature against composition at one pressure

The same picture turned upside down, because a compound with a higher vapor pressure has a lower boiling point. Liquid is now at the bottom. This is the diagram used for distillation, which runs at fixed pressure.

xy: vapor against liquid composition

Each point is one liquid and its equilibrium vapor. The further the curve bends away from the diagonal, the easier the separation.

Azeotrope

At an azeotrope the liquid and vapor have the same composition, so boiling no longer separates the mixture. The bubble and dew curves touch, and the xy curve crosses the diagonal. With an ideal-gas vapor this happens where \(\gamma_1 P_1^{\mathrm{sat}} = \gamma_2 P_2^{\mathrm{sat}}\). Positive deviations give a pressure maximum (a minimum boiling point), negative deviations the opposite. The solver detects azeotropes and marks them.

The xy diagram of ethanol–water in the calculator

The P–T diagram

Hold the composition fixed and change the temperature. A pure compound has a single vapor-pressure curve. A mixture has two: the pressure at which a liquid of that composition starts to boil, and the lower pressure at which a vapor of the same composition starts to condense. Between them the mixture is partly liquid and partly vapor. With an equation of state, the two curves approach each other toward the mixture's critical point.

What you may choose

The calculator always asks for one variable to hold constant. The reason is the phase rule. Counting the variables of each phase and subtracting the equilibrium conditions leaves

\[ F = n + 2 - \pi \]

independent variables, for \(n\) compounds in \(\pi\) phases. Two compounds in two phases gives \(F = 2\). Fix one variable (the temperature, say), sweep a second (the liquid composition), and everything else follows: pressure, vapor composition, fugacities.

The Python program applies the same count to any input, to decide whether a problem is fully specified, underspecified or overspecified. An external field adds one more variable.

Solids and fields

The calculator page covers vapor–liquid equilibrium. The Python program applies the same equal-fugacity idea in two more directions.

Solids

A pure solid has a fugacity too. Relative to the pure liquid, it follows from the melting point and the enthalpy of fusion:

\[ \ln\frac{f_i^{S}}{f_i^{L}} = -\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{fus}}}{R}\left(\frac1T - \frac1{T_m}\right) . \]

Setting this equal to the liquid-mixture fugacity \(x_i\gamma_i f_i^{L}\) gives freezing and solubility curves, and the eutectic where two solids form together.

Gravity and centrifugal fields

In a field, a molecule also has potential energy, so the quantity that must be uniform becomes \(\mu_i + M_i\Phi\), with \(M_i\) the molar mass and \(\Phi\) the potential (\(gz\) for gravity). In fugacity terms,

\[ \ln\hat f_i + \frac{M_i\,\Phi}{RT} = \text{constant throughout the system}. \]

Two phases touching at one height share the same \(\Phi\), so the phase diagram at any one position is unchanged. What the field changes is how pressure and composition vary from place to place.
