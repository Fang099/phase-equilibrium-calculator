ThreeTwo equations of state

examples/propane_butane_pr_pxy.txt gives four constants per compound and asks for a Pxy diagram at 300 K with Peng–Robinson. At \(x_{\text{propane}} = 0.4\) the bubble pressure is bar, and the first vapor contains propane.

The ends of the diagram are the vapor pressures of the pure compounds, so the threetwo equations can be compared on pure propane:

A complete phase diagram from latent heats

examples/benzene_naphthalene.txt contains no critical constants and no vapor-pressure equation, only boiling points, melting points and the two latent heats of each compound:

components = benzene, naphthalene
P = 1 atm
sweep = x
benzene.Tb = 353.24 K
benzene.dHvap = 30.72 kJ/mol
benzene.Tm = 278.68 K
benzene.dHfus = 9.87 kJ/mol
naphthalene.Tb = 491.14 K
naphthalene.dHvap = 43.2 kJ/mol
naphthalene.Tm = 353.43 K
naphthalene.dHfus = 19.01 kJ/mol

The checker finds three equilibria it can calculate from this: boiling, benzene crystallizing and naphthalene crystallizing. The solver adds the eutectic where both solids form together: K at \(x_{\text{benzene}}\) = , close to the measured value of about 270 K.

An azeotrope from the database

examples/database_ethanol_water_nrtl.txt asks for ethanol and water at 1 atm with NRTL and lets the database supply everything else: the Antoine constants and the three NRTL parameters measured for this pair (DECHEMA, via ChemSep). The solver finds the azeotrope at \(x_{\text{ethanol}}\) = and K. The measured one is at about 0.894 and 351.3 K.

Pure fluids: vapor pressure and a column in gravity

examples/pure_water_vapor_pressure.txt asks for water's vapor pressure at 373.15 K with Peng–Robinson; examples/pure_propane_gravity_column.txt puts propane at 300 K in a 20 m column with 10.0 bar at the bottom. The vapor pressure there is bar, so the bottom is liquid ( kg/m³); the fugacity falls with height and reaches its saturation value at m, which is where the liquid ends: close to \((P_0 - P^{\mathrm{sat}})/\rho_L g\), as hydrostatics says. examples/pure_co2_missing_data.txt shows the checker listing what a centrifuge calculation still needs.

How well do the three equations of state give pure-compound vapor pressures? The table compares their normal boiling points with the Antoine correlation (a fit to measured vapor pressures), and their vapor pressure at \(T = 0.7\,T_c\) (the temperature that defines the acentric factor):

van der Waals has no acentric factor and misses by a factor of several; SRK and Peng–Robinson bring in the acentric factor, which is defined by the vapor pressure at \(0.7\,T_c\), so they land within about 1% there and within a degree or two of the normal boiling point. The comparison over the whole saturation curve, against reference equations of state, is in the validation section. Water is the hardest case (Peng–Robinson boils it at 374.6 K), and the liquid densities are poorer than the pressures: Peng–Robinson gives water about 800 kg/m³ at its boiling point instead of 958.

Validation against measured and reference data

scripts/validate_real_data.py compares the models with data that the program was not built from; the data files are in validation/. For pure fluids the reference is each fluid's reference equation of state (IAPWS-95 for water, Span–Wagner for CO₂, Lemmon et al. for propane, and so on), which reproduces the measured vapor pressures and densities to within their uncertainty; it is evaluated at ten temperatures from 0.5 Tc to 0.95 Tc. For mixtures the reference is measured VLE from the literature, taken from the NIST TRC ThermoML archive.

Pure fluids: average absolute deviation (%) along the saturation curve

Peng–Robinson gets the vapor pressure within about 2% for nonpolar fluids and 1–6% for polar ones, but its liquid density is 13–19% low for water, methanol and acetone. van der Waals overestimates the vapor pressure by a large factor at low temperature.

Binary VLE at 101.3 kPa: NRTL against measurements

Methane–ethane, 126–140 K: Peng–Robinson against 59 measured points

The E-PPR78 \(k_{12}(T)\) gives the smallest pressure error and almost no bias; the fixed DECHEMA value underpredicts the pressure. Data: Han et al., J. Chem. Eng. Data 2012, 57, 3242.

The field: Peng–Robinson and SRK against the reference equations of state in the same field

No measured phase boundaries in a laboratory-scale field were found, so the reference equations were integrated through the same field. The propane band width is within % on average with PR and % with SRK, the same order as their liquid-density errors. The interface height depends strongly on the vapor pressure: with the same absolute bottom pressure PR puts it twice as high as the reference, because its vapor pressure is 0.3% low; with the same excess pressure above each model's own vapor pressure they agree within 4%.

Three and four components

The solver works with any number of compounds; a flash is the natural question for more than two. examples/acetone_methanol_water_flash.txt splits an acetone–methanol–water feed at 345 K and 1 atm with NRTL, all three pairs from the database. examples/natural_gas_flash_ppr78.txt splits a methane–ethane–propane–n-butane feed at 250 K and 30 bar with Peng–Robinson, every \(k_{ij}\) predicted by E-PPR78 at 250 K. The same examples are in the calculator's "Three or four components" panel, with a triangle diagram for the ternary.

In each case the feed lies between the liquid and the vapor (\(z_i = (1-\beta)x_i + \beta y_i\) for every compound) and the temperature lies between the bubble and dew temperatures of the feed, as it must for two phases. The lightest compound is enriched in the vapor (\(K > 1\)), the heaviest in the liquid.

Using the database from an input file

One line, database = builtin, fills in every property the file does not give. examples/database_ethanol_water_nrtl.txt is the whole input for the diagram the calculator opens with:

components = ethanol, water
database = builtin
activity = nrtl
P = 1 atm
sweep = x

Anything written in the file overrides the database, so a single value can be replaced without retyping the rest. examples/database_pt_diagram.txt uses x(L) = 0.5 with sweep = T to ask for a P–T diagram at fixed composition.

One line, database = builtin, fills in every property the file does not give. examples/database_pt_diagram.txt is the whole input for a P–T diagram of an equimolar propane–n-butane mixture with Peng–Robinson:

components = propane, n-butane
database = builtin
model = PR
x(L) = 0.5
sweep = T

The database supplies \(T_c\), \(P_c\), \(\omega\), the molar masses and \(k_{12}\). Anything written in the file overrides it, so a single value can be replaced without retyping the rest.

A 500 m column in gravity

examples/gravity_column_gas_cap.txt puts a liquid with \(z_{\text{propane}} = 0.4\) at 320 K and 25 bar at the bottom of a 500 m column. Going up, the pressure falls. At m it reaches the local bubble pressure ( bar) and a gas cap forms above.

The solver never uses the hydrostatic equation \(dP = -\rho g\,dz\). It only requires \(\ln\hat f_i + M_i g z/RT\) to be constant, and the pressure profile that comes out satisfies the hydrostatic equation to about \(10^{-7}\) bar.

A centrifuge

examples/ethanol_water_centrifuge.txt spins an ethanol–water liquid at 20,000 rpm. At the inner radius, 0.05 m, the liquid is at its bubble point (\(x_{\text{ethanol}} = 0.5\), 350 K, bar) in contact with its vapor. It fills the rotor out to 0.10 m.

Radius (m)Pressure (bar)\(x_{\text{ethanol}}\)

At the wall the liquid is at about bar and slightly depleted in ethanol, the lighter compound. The vapor–liquid diagram at any one radius is the ordinary one: the field changes where each state is found, not the equilibrium between phases that touch.
